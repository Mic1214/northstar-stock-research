Important limitations and evidence rules
  • Some checks need data that SEC filings alone may not provide, including analyst estimates and price targets, peer comparisons, institutional ownership trends, and distribution days.
  • Record the source and date in evidence notes. Do not treat an assumption, an old estimate, or unavailable information as a confirmed pass.
  • Not yet verified is distinct from Not met. It keeps the final label provisional until the evidence has been checked.
  • This is an educational research framework, not personalized financial advice. It helps organize investigation; it cannot guarantee a profitable result.

01 / BASICSbasics

What is a stock?

A stock represents a share of ownership in a company. Its value can rise or fall, and owning a share does not guarantee a profit.

Question to think about

What rights might ownership provide, and what could make the value change?

Investor.gov: Stocks ↗
02 / FINANCIALSfinancials

Revenue vs. profit

Revenue is what a business earns from sales before expenses. Profit is what remains after the expenses included in that calculation are subtracted.

Question to think about

Why can sales grow while profit shrinks?

SEC: Financial statements guide ↗
03 / FINANCIALSfinancials

Three financial statements

An income statement summarizes results over a period. A balance sheet shows assets, liabilities, and owners’ equity at a point in time. A cash-flow statement tracks cash moving in and out.

Question to think about

Why would one statement rarely tell the whole story?

SEC: Financial statements guide ↗
04 / FINANCIALSfinancials

Cash flow vs. profit

Profit and cash flow are related, but they are not the same. Accounting income can include sales not yet paid for and non-cash expenses; cash flow helps show whether cash is actually coming in or going out.

Question to think about

Why might a business report a profit but still struggle to pay a bill on time?

SEC: Financial statements guide ↗
05 / FINANCIALSfinancials

Debt and obligations

Debt is money a business has borrowed and generally must repay, often with interest. Debt is not automatically bad; the amount, cost, timing, and ability to repay all matter.

Question to think about

What information would help you judge whether debt is manageable?

SEC: Financial statements guide ↗
06 / VALUATIONvaluation

Market capitalization

Market capitalization is the total market value of a company’s outstanding shares. It is commonly calculated by multiplying the current share price by the number of shares outstanding. Size alone does not tell you whether something is fairly priced.

Question to think about

Why is a company’s size different from how expensive its shares are relative to its earnings?

Investor.gov: Stocks ↗
07 / FINANCIALSfinancials

Earnings per share (EPS)

Earnings per share relates a company’s profit attributable to common shareholders to the number of shares used in the calculation. Different EPS versions exist, so comparisons should use the same type and reporting period.

Question to think about

Why might rising EPS alone not prove that a business is getting healthier?

SEC: Financial statements guide ↗
08 / VALUATIONvaluation

Price-to-earnings ratio

The P/E ratio compares a share price with earnings per share. It is one clue about valuation, not a verdict. It can be misleading when earnings are negative, unusually high, or not comparable.

Question to think about

What other evidence would you want before interpreting a P/E ratio?

Investor.gov: Investment products ↗
09 / BASICSbasics

Dividends

A dividend is a distribution a company may choose to pay to its shareholders. Dividends are not guaranteed; a company can change or stop them, and a payment does not prevent the share price from falling.

Question to think about

Why should a dividend payment be considered alongside the business’s financial health?

Investor.gov: Stocks ↗
10 / RISKrisk

Risk and return

Return is the gain or loss from an investment over a period. Risk includes the possibility that the result differs from what you expected, including losing money. A promised high return with little or no risk is a warning sign.

Question to think about

What could go wrong even when an idea sounds convincing?

Investor.gov: Introduction to investing ↗
11 / RISKrisk

Diversification

Diversification spreads exposure across different investments or asset types so results depend less on any one holding. It can reduce some risks, but it cannot eliminate the risk of a broad market decline.

Question to think about

Could several different-looking investments still depend on the same risk?

Investor.gov: Asset allocation and diversification ↗
12 / RISKrisk

Volatility

Volatility describes how much and how quickly a price or return changes over time. High volatility can mean larger swings in either direction; it does not tell you which direction comes next.

Question to think about

Why is a large recent price move not, by itself, a reliable forecast?

Investor.gov: Introduction to investing ↗
13 / PLANNINGplanning

Compound growth

Compound growth happens when returns build on the starting amount and on earlier returns that remain invested. The result depends on time, contributions, fees, and actual returns; growth is not guaranteed.

Question to think about

How could fees or a long period of losses affect a compounding result?

Investor.gov: Introduction to investing ↗
14 / PLANNINGplanning

Fees and expenses

Trading, account, fund, or advisory fees can reduce what you keep. A small recurring cost can add up over time, so check what a service charges and how the charge is calculated.

Question to think about

How would you compare two options after accounting for their total costs?

Investor.gov: Investment products ↗
15 / PLANNINGplanning

Liquidity

Liquidity describes how readily something can be converted to cash without a large price concession or delay. An investment that is difficult to sell may not suit money needed soon.

Question to think about

Why does the time when you need money affect which risks are acceptable?

Investor.gov: Investment products ↗
16 / PLANNINGplanning

Time horizon

A time horizon is how long you expect to keep money invested before needing it. Longer timelines can give more time to recover from some downturns, but they do not guarantee a recovery or remove risk.

Question to think about

Why might money needed next year be treated differently from money intended for decades later?

Investor.gov: Introduction to investing ↗
17 / PLANNINGplanning

Inflation and purchasing power

Inflation is a broad rise in prices over time. When prices rise, the same amount of money generally buys fewer goods and services, so it is useful to think about returns after inflation as well as before it.

Question to think about

If your money grows by less than the price level, has its purchasing power grown?

U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics: CPI ↗
18 / RESEARCHbasics

Check the evidence

Before trusting a financial claim, look for the original source, the date, the reporting period, and the exact definition being used. Separate confirmed facts from interpretations, forecasts, and opinions.

Question to think about

Could you independently verify the most important claim from an original document?

SEC: Financial statements guide ↗
